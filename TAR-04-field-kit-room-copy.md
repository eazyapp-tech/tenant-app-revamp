## 0. Read this first

### The rules

1. Never go hunting for bugs. We can read the code and we already know what is broken. Ask about their life, not about our defects.
2. Nobody is asked to design. Stories, recalls, counts, choices. We translate.
3. Every question says what kind of answer you are after. **[TODAY]** how it is now · **[NO LIMITS]** anything is possible, let them imagine · **[PICK]** read them the choices and make them choose · **[MUST]** ask it even if the session collapses. The nineteen must-asks alone run about twenty-two minutes and are what let one session be compared with another. A few lines are instructions to you rather than questions, and carry no brackets at all.
4. Ideas are harvested as needs. Every volunteered feature gets the follow-up: what would that let you do?
5. Order is fixed: their experience, then specific moments, then their wishes, then our concepts, then ranking. Theirs before ours.
6. Banned: is it easy, what would you improve, any issues, would you use this. Instead: when did you last need it.
7. **The English below is the intent, never the script.** You ask in their language, in the way they talk, in your own natural words. If you find yourself reading a sentence aloud from this page, stop and say it the way you would say it to a friend. If a question needs our vocabulary to be understood, the question is wrong.
8. Pilot on two people, fix what confused them, then scale.
9. Log every tenant's age band. Under 18: a guardian says yes before the session, in a voice note or a written message you keep, and the session's row records that it exists and its date. Their session skips every offer and money concept test. Their answers about food, the gate, complaints and daily life are among the most valuable we will collect.
10. Gender context: entry, attendance and family questions are handled with care. Never suggest protection and never suggest watching. Note the word they choose.
11. Record every session. Consent first. Ledger extracted the same day.
12. Everything you write down names the session it came from. No session, no insight: it goes on a separate list of things we merely believe.

### Before any real session

Three gates, in order. Skipping them is how good questions produce useless answers.

1. **Write the spoken script**, one per language you will actually use, with someone who speaks it daily. Not a translation of these sentences: the same intent, said the way that person would say it. Hindi first, then whatever the properties need. The English here stays the source of truth and does not change when a script does.
2. **Pilot on two people per instrument.** Fix whatever confused them. A question that needed explaining gets rewritten, not repeated louder.
3. **Sanchay sits in on both tenant practice sessions.** After that you run alone.

### Three session lengths, and what comes out of each

That is longer than most people will sit, so the length is chosen before the session, not discovered halfway through it. Never improvise the cut in the room: a section abandoned mid-flow produces answers that cannot be compared with anyone else's.

### What people get for their time

Ruled by Sanchay, 8 Sep 2026: **three hundred rupees for every tenant session**, by UPI or recharge, and **two hundred more** for anyone who completes the three-day diary. Owners, managers and internal staff get nothing, because the research serves them directly. Budget for the round is about six thousand rupees, or six and a half if all six diaries run.

Three rules go with it, and they matter more than the number.

- **Flat for everyone, never scaled.** The amount is the same for a student and for a cash-first tenant. Paying some people less because they have less is worse than the problem it solves.
- **Three hundred, not five.** Five hundred is close to a day's wage for the cash-first and blue-collar slots, and at that point it stops being a thank-you and becomes a reason to say whatever keeps the interviewer happy. Three hundred is a courtesy to everyone and a day's pay to nobody.
- **Never mentioned at recruitment. Handed over at the end, unconditionally.** Say plainly that it is for the time, not for the answers. Someone who ends the session angry with the app gets it exactly the same way as someone who ends it pleased.

The reason we pay at all is the four slots for tenants who never installed the app or uninstalled it. They are the least interested in helping us and they hold the finding that explains near-zero adoption. An unpaid hour is how that group goes missing from the sample.

### What the interviewer does not do

The risk here is a particular one: the person asking these questions is the person who designed the app. When someone criticises it, the pull to explain is enormous, and explaining ends the answer.

- **Do not defend the app.** Not once, not lightly, not at the end.
- **Do not explain what a screen is for.** If they misread it, that misreading is the finding.
- **Do not promise anything.** Not a fix, not a feature, not a timeline.
- **Do not fill a silence.** Count to five. The sentence after the pause is usually the real one.
- **Do not finish their sentence**, and do not offer them a word when they are searching for one. Their word is the data; yours is a leading question wearing a helpful face.
- If they ask you directly what the app will do, say the truth: you are here to listen, and decisions come later.

### Consent, said before recording starts

Say it in their language, plainly: I am recording so I can write your words down correctly, not from memory. Your name goes nowhere. You can skip anything, and you can stop whenever you like. Is that alright?

### Same-day discipline

Within the same day: listen back, fill in the capture sheet in section 2 in full, and do not stop at the quotes. A session you have not written up within 48 hours is marked on its row as memory only.

### When to stop

Seventeen sessions is a plan, not a rule. Stop a slot when two sessions in a row produce no insight you have not already written down. Stop the round when that is true of the sample as a whole, even if it happens at twelve, and write the synthesis then. Running sessions past the point of learning costs the schedule and teaches nothing. Running fewer than eight tenant sessions is not a round; it is a set of anecdotes.

## 3. Instrument A · Tenant (71, 50, or 22 minutes, decided before you arrive)

> **Say this before you press record, in their language.** I am recording so I can write your words down correctly, not from memory. Your name goes nowhere. You can skip anything, and you can stop whenever you like. Is that alright?
>
> **The six things you never do.** Do not defend the app. Do not explain what a screen is for. Do not promise anything. Do not fill a silence, count to five. Do not finish their sentence or hand them a word. If they ask what the app will do, say you are here to listen and decisions come later.
>
> **When the session does not go to plan.**
> - **They say no to recording.** Say that is completely fine, put the phone away where they can see it, and run the 22-minute floor on paper. Write their words, not your summary. Note "no recording" on the session's row in the capture sheet. Never ask twice.
> - **The owner or a staff member stays in the room.** Ask warmly for ten minutes one to one so the tenant can speak freely. If they will not leave, run the session anyway, note "staff present" on the session's row, and treat every answer about money and complaints as suspect.
> - **They answer in one word.** Do not rephrase the question. Ask when it last happened. A story restarts a person; a rephrased question closes them further.
> - **They ask you to fix their problem.** Say you will pass it to the owner and that you have no power to fix it yourself. Then actually pass it on, once, after the session. Never trade a fix for an answer.
> - **They have to leave halfway.** Do not race. Go straight to the must-ask questions you have not reached, in order, and stop. Note where you stopped.
> - **Someone under 18 turns up and the guardian has not said yes.** No consent in hand, no session. Ask the owner to get the voice note and rebook.
> - **They walk out early.** They get the three hundred rupees anyway. It was for the time, not the answers.

## 6. Diary and shadow visit

**Diary.** Four to six tenants, three days, over WhatsApp. Each day: two photographs, morning and evening, and one line answering what the biggest task or bother tied to this place was today. Day three adds: who did you message about this place this week, and what about. The photographs are the raw material for the written visual brief, and you are the one who writes that brief, from these pictures and from the life-texture answers in A6.7. Every line of it has to point at a session; a line with no session behind it gets struck. Consent for the diary is taken at Q59, the last line of the close (A9). Thank them daily and never prompt beyond the script.

**Shadow visit.** One property, half a day. Arrive before people come back in the evening, leave after dinner. Sit where tenants pass: the gate, the mess, the common room. Log every interaction about the property you see: who, with whom, over what channel, about what, how long it took. No interviews. The point is to see how the day actually passes, watched instead of asked.
